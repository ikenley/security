Security

No deep thoughts.

  1. Prioritize critical resources
    1. Prioritize Apple/Google/Microsoft account, your password manager, your mobile carrier, banks and brokerages
    2. For engineers: GitHub, AWS root accounts, domain registrars.
  2. Use a password manager. Use a unique password for every account. Choose one of these:
    1. Cloud-hosted managers (Bitwarden, 1Password, Proton Pass, and similar)
    2. Browser managers: Google Password Manager / Apple Passwords
    3. https://keepass.info/ synced to Dropbox
  3. Enroll in multi-factor authentication (MFA)
    1. Register two hardware security keys (a daily one and a backup kept elsewhere), or passkeys on at least two devices
    2. Use an authenticator app (Google Authenticator) where passkeys aren’t supported.
    3. Remove SMS as a second factor wherever possible, since it’s vulnerable to SIM swapping.
  4. Enroll in Google’s Advanced Protection Program
  5. Your account is only as secure as its weakest backup sign-in.
    1. Go to myaccount.google.com/security and remove SMS codes, the recovery phone and old recovery emails. Keep only security keys, passkeys and printed backup codes.
    2. Store recovery codes offline, for example printed and kept with important documents.
  6. Build habits for social engineering.
    1. Treat urgency as a red flag in itself.
    2. Verify unexpected requests for money, codes, or access through a separate channel you initiate.
    3. Never approve an MFA prompt you didn’t trigger.
    4. Don’t click on links in emails you didn’t expect, especially ones asking you to log in, pay, or “verify.” Go to the site yourself instead.
    5. Agree on a family code word. Voice-cloning scams that impersonate a relative are now cheap to run.
      1. Unguessable from the outside. Avoid pet names, street names, school mascots, favorite teams, or anything visible in photos or social media posts. Scammers mine exactly that material.
      2. Memorable for everyone. That includes grandparents, who are frequent targets of these scams, and kids once they’re old enough.
      3. Odd enough not to come up by accident. A random two-word combination works well.
      4. Never written in texts or email. Agree on it in person, so it can’t leak from a compromised inbox.
      5. A private memory is often the stickiest choice, because it’s easy to recall under stress and meaningless to outsiders.
  7. Randomize your security question answers
    1. Instead of a real answer, create another password manager value
  8. Freeze your credit
    1. https://www.experian.com/help/credit-freeze/
    2. https://www.equifax.com/personal/credit-report-services/credit-freeze/
    3. https://www.transunion.com/credit-freeze
  9. Use a secure message app
    1. Look for end-to-end encryption: Signal, iMessage, or WhatsApp
    2. Turn on its registration lock PIN
  10. Set up an email alias and use it for sign-ups
